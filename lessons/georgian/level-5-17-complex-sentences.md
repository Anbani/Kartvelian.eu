## 1. The Core Connectors: And, But, Or

You likely already know these from daily practice, but let's officially formalize the big three.

| Georgian | Transliteration | English | Example Sentence |
|----------|-----------------|---------|------------------|
| **და** | da | And | მე და შენ მივდივართ. (*Me da shen mivdivart.* - You and I are going.) |
| **მაგრამ** | magram | But | მინდა, მაგრამ არ შემიძლია. (*Minda, magram ar shemidzlia.* - I want to, but I cannot.) |
| **ან** | an | Or | ჩაი ან ყავა გინდა? (*Chai an q'ava ginda?* - Do you want tea or coffee?) |

## 2. Cause and Effect: "Because" and "Therefore"

When you want to explain *why* something happened, you need these two vital conjunctions. 

### A. Because: `იმიტომ, რომ` (imit'om, rom)
In Georgian, "because" is actually a two-part phrase. **იმიტომ** (*imit'om*) means "for that reason," and **რომ** (*rom*) means "that." 

> **🚨 Punctuation Rule:** In written Georgian, the comma *always* goes strictly before the word `რომ` (*rom*), separating the two parts of the conjunction.

*   **ქართულს ვსწავლობ, იმიტომ, რომ თბილისში ვცხოვრობ.**
    *   *(kartuls vsts'avlob, imit'om, rom Tbilisshi vtskhovrob)*
    *   I am learning Georgian because I live in Tbilisi.
*   **გუშინ არ მოვედი, იმიტომ, რომ ავად ვიყავი.**
    *   *(gushin ar movedi, imit'om, rom avad viq'avi)*
    *   Yesterday I didn't come because I was sick.

### B. Therefore / So: `ამიტომ` (amit'om)
If you want to flip the sentence and state the cause first, followed by the result, you use **ამიტომ** (*amit'om*).

*   **თბილისში ვცხოვრობ, ამიტომ ქართულს ვსწავლობ.**
    *   *(Tbilisshi vtskhovrob, amit'om kartuls vsts'avlob)*
    *   I live in Tbilisi; therefore, I am learning Georgian.
*   **ავად ვიყავი, ამიტომ სახლში დავრჩი.**
    *   *(avad viq'avi, amit'om sakhlshi davrchi)*
    *   I was sick, so I stayed at home.

## 3. Contrast and Concession: "Although"

While **მაგრამ** (*magram* - but) is great for simple contrasts, B1 speakers frequently use **თუმცა** (tumtsa) to add nuance, meaning "although" or "however." 

*   **ქართული ენა რთულია, თუმცა ძალიან ლამაზია.** 
    *   *(kartuli ena rtulia, tumtsa dzalian lamazia)*
    *   The Georgian language is difficult, although it is very beautiful.
*   **წვიმს, თუმცა მაინც გარეთ გავალ.**
    *   *(ts'vims, tumtsa maints garet gaval)*
    *   It is raining; however, I will still go outside.

## 4. The Mighty "That": `რომ` (rom)

We have already seen `რომ` (*rom*) as part of "because", but it is also used entirely on its own as the conjunction "that." It bridges a thought/statement to the action. You will use this constantly with verbs like *to know* (ვიცი, *vitsi*), *to think* (ვფიქრობ, *vpikrob*), and *to say* (ვამბობ, *vambob*).

*   **მე ვიცი, რომ სახლში ხარ.** 
    *   *(me vitsi, rom sakhlshi khar)*
    *   I know **that** you are at home.
*   **მან მითხრა, რომ ხვალ მოვა.**
    *   *(man mitkhra, rom khval mova)*
    *   He/She told me **that** he/she will come tomorrow.
*   **ვფიქრობ, რომ ეს კარგი იდეაა.**
    *   *(vpikrob, rom es k'argi ideaa)*
    *   I think **that** this is a good idea.

## 5. Relative Clauses: "Which" & "Who" (`რომელიც`, *romelits*)

How do you say "The man *who* is reading" or "The book *which* is on the table"? 

In English, we use "who" for people and "which/that" for objects. In Georgian, you use one universal relative pronoun[^2]: **რომელიც (romelits)**.

You use `რომელიც` (*romelits*) to embed a descriptive clause right next to the noun[^3] it describes.

*   **კაცი, რომელიც კითხულობს, ჩემი ძმაა.** 
    *   *(k'atsi, romelits k'itkhulobs, chemi dzmaa)*
    *   The man **who** is reading is my brother.
*   **წიგნი, რომელიც მაგიდაზეა, ჩემია.**
    *   *(ts'igni, romelits magidazea, chemia)*
    *   The book **which** is on the table is mine.
*   **მანქანა, რომელიც ვიყიდე, ძალიან სწრაფად დადის.**
    *   *(mankana, romelits viq'ide, dzalian sts'rapad dadis)*
    *   The car **that** I bought drives very fast.

> **🧠 Advanced Grammar Note:** Because `რომელიც` (*romelits*) ends in an `-ი` (*-i*), it is technically in the Nominative case[^1]. Just like any other noun, it can change cases (to Ergative `რომელმაც` *romelmats* or Dative `რომელსაც` *romelsats*) depending on its grammatical role inside the relative clause. As you read more Georgian, you will see these declined forms in action, but for now, practice the standard Nominative form!

## 6. Practice Dialogue: Putting the Pieces Together

Read this conversation. Notice how the speakers connect their thoughts using the new vocabulary. They no longer sound like beginners; they sound conversational and expressive.

**დათო (Dato):** სარა, რატომ სწავლობ ქართულს? (Sara, rat'om sts'avlob kartuls? - *Sara, why are you learning Georgian?*)
**სარა (Sara):** **იმიტომ, რომ** თბილისში ვცხოვრობ. მე **ვიცი, რომ** რთული ენაა, **თუმცა** ძალიან მომწონს. (**Imit'om, rom** Tbilisshi vtskhovrob. Me **vitsi, rom** rtuli enaa, **tumtsa** dzalian momts'ons. - ***Because*** *I live in Tbilisi. I **know that** it is a difficult language, **although** I like it very much.*)
**დათო (Dato):** ეგ წიგნი, **რომელიც** მაგიდაზეა, შენია? (Eg ts'igni, **romelits** magidazea, shenia? - *Is that book **which** is on the table yours?*)
**სარა (Sara):** კი. ქართული გრამატიკაა, **ამიტომ** ყოველდღე ვკითხულობ. (K'i. Kartuli gramat'ik'aa, **amit'om** q'oveldghe vk'itkhulob. - *Yes. It is Georgian grammar; **therefore**, I read it every day.*)

### Next Steps

### Grammar terms used in this lesson

[^1]: **Nominative case** (სახელობითი ბრუნვა, *sakhelobiti brunva*) — The basic, dictionary form of a noun, typically used to mark the subject of a sentence.
[^2]: **Pronoun** (ნაცვალსახელი, *natsvalsakheli*) — A word that takes the place of a noun.
[^3]: **Noun** (არსებითი სახელი, *arsebiti sakheli*) — A word that represents a person, place, thing, or idea.
